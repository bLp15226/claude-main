# Augur Doctrine — Customer Conversation Methodology (Master Report: Fitzpatrick Synthesis)

**Source basis:** Original synthesis of the customer-development conversation methodology popularized by Rob Fitzpatrick (*The Mom Test*, 2013). This is NOT an extraction of the book — the book is in copyright. This file distills the framework in original language, cross-referenced with the Schwartz awareness/sophistication model already in the stack, and adapted to PHC's faceless-luxury dropshipping context.

**Standing rule:** All research conduct must respect PHC ethics (no fabricated testimonials, no fake urgency, no manufactured social proof, honest reason-why). Conflicts flagged inline.

**Who runs this doctrine:** Augur owns it. Scriptor consumes its outputs (voice-of-customer language). Vesta inherits the interview mechanics when she's forged.

## 1\. The Core Problem: Everyone Lies to Be Nice

The foundational insight: when you ask people about your business idea, they lie to you — not maliciously, but socially. Your mom says your app idea is great because she loves you. Strangers say it's cool because ending the conversation politely is cheaper than critiquing you. Enthusiasm in a conversation about a hypothetical product is *worthless as data*.

**The corollary that matters most:** the lie is triggered by *you*, not by them. If your question mentions your idea, invites opinions about the future, or fishes for validation, you have contaminated the sample. The fix is never "find more honest people." The fix is asking questions that are impossible to answer with a polite lie.

**Augur standing rule:** Any research finding sourced from a conversation where PHC's product was pitched first is tagged `CONTAMINATED` and weighted near zero. Compliments are not signal. Excitement is not signal. Money, time, and past behavior are signal.

## 2\. The Three Laws of Clean Questions

Every customer question Augur designs — interview scripts, post-purchase surveys, DM conversations, comment-section probes — passes all three:

**Law 1 — Their life, not your idea.** Talk about the customer's world: their routines, frustrations, purchases, workarounds. The product stays out of the conversation as long as possible. You learn whether a problem exists and how much it costs them; you do not ask them to imagine your solution.

**Law 2 — Specific past, not hypothetical future.** "Would you buy X?" invites a fantasy answer from their idealized self — the self that exercises daily and reads more. "Walk me through the last time you dealt with X" retrieves a fact. People are terrible predictors of their own behavior and excellent (if incomplete) reporters of what they actually did. Every future-tense question gets rewritten into past tense before it ships.

**Law 3 — Listen more than you talk.** The moment you start explaining, defending, or pitching, data collection stops and performance begins. The interviewer's job is to shut up, follow the thread, and dig. Rule of thumb: if you talked more than a third of the time, you ran a pitch, not an interview.

**Fast quality test for any question:** could a polite stranger answer it truthfully without hurting your feelings? If honesty and politeness conflict in the question's design, redesign the question.

## 3\. Bad Data Taxonomy (What to Discard)

Three categories of worthless data, and the deflection move for each:

**3.1 Compliments.** "That's really cool," "I love it," "I'd definitely use that." Fool's gold. When you get one, deflect back to their life: "Thanks — so when's the last time this actually came up for you?" A compliment recorded in research notes without a deflection attempt is an interviewer error, not a finding.

**3.2 Fluff.** Three sub-species:

- *Generics:* "I usually / I always / I never..." — averages people report about themselves are fiction. Anchor to an instance: "When did that last happen? What did you do?"  
- *Hypotheticals:* "I would / I might / I could see myself..." — future-self fantasy. Rewrite to past tense on the spot.  
- *Wishes:* "I'd love a thing that..." — desire without cost. Follow with: "What are you doing about it today? What have you tried? What did that cost you?"

Fluff is dangerous specifically because it *feels* like validation. It's the most common source of false-positive product decisions.

**3.3 Ideas and feature requests.** When a customer proposes a feature or pivots your concept, the request itself is not the data — the *motivation underneath it* is. Dig: "Why do you want that? What would it let you do? How are you coping without it?" Sometimes the dig reveals a real burning problem; usually it reveals a passing thought. Ideas go in the notes as symptoms, never as roadmap items.

**3.4 The ego trap (interviewer-side bad data).** Seeking approval — even unconsciously — corrupts everything. The tell: feeling good after a meeting full of compliments. The doctrine position: a conversation that ends in praise and nothing else *failed*. You want truth, including the truth that nobody cares. Learning "this product is dead" in one week of conversations is a *win* — it's the cheapest possible price for that information.

## 4\. Does the Problem Actually Matter? (Ranking Before Building)

A problem existing is not enough — it has to matter enough to displace money and attention. Diagnostic sequence for any problem PHC's products claim to solve:

1. **Does it come up unprompted?** If you have to remind them the problem exists, it doesn't rank.  
2. **Where does it rank against their other annoyances?** "What are the three most frustrating parts of \[routine\]?" — if your problem isn't volunteered, note that.  
3. **Have they tried to solve it?** Someone who has never Googled a fix, bought a competitor, or rigged a workaround does not care enough to buy yours. Past attempted solutions are the single strongest predictor of purchase.  
4. **What does the current coping cost them?** Money spent, time lost, embarrassment felt. Cost of the status quo ≈ ceiling on willingness to pay.

**PHC translation:** for a $60 VeloCurl-class product, the bar is lower than SaaS — impulse-adjacent beauty purchases don't require a burning problem. But the *ranking* logic still governs which angle Scriptor leads with: the frustration customers volunteer first, in their words, is the lead. The frustration we wish they had is not.

## 5\. Keep It Casual, Keep It Cheap

Formal "customer interviews" with calendar invites and slide decks create performance pressure on both sides and cost hours. The alternative: strip the meeting out of the learning. Three good questions woven into a natural conversation — at a school pickup, in a Facebook group thread, in a post-purchase DM — beat an hour-long scheduled interview.

- Early-stage learning should feel like genuine curiosity about their life, because it is.  
- Speed matters: the value of this research is front-loaded. Five scrappy conversations this week outrank twenty formal ones next month.  
- **Ethics note (PHC):** casual does not mean covert. If asked "why do you want to know?", the honest answer ("I run a small home-goods brand and I'm trying to understand this before I sell anything") is given straight. Curiosity without disclosure-on-request would violate the spirit of the transparency ethos. This is a deliberate tightening of the source framework.

## 6\. Commitment and Advancement (The Currency Test)

Once conversations *do* involve the product (later stage), the only meaningful positive outcome is the other person giving something up. Three currencies:

- **Time:** a scheduled follow-up with a stated agenda, a lengthy feedback session on a prototype, joining a beta.  
- **Reputation:** an intro to their boss, their team, a friend with the same problem; a public endorsement under their own name.  
- **Money:** a deposit, a pre-order, a letter of intent, an actual purchase.

A meeting that ends with "sounds great, keep me posted" produced *nothing* — that's a polite no. Doctrine terminology: every product conversation must end in either **advancement** (they paid a currency) or a **clear rejection** (also valuable — it's data). The mushy middle is the failure state, and it's usually the interviewer's fault for not asking for a concrete next step.

**PHC translation:** the Founding 50 at-cost offer is a purpose-built commitment test — money is the currency, and the honest reason-why (real reviews from real customers) satisfies the ethics rule. Email signups are a *weak* currency (near-zero cost); treat list growth as interest, not validation. A pre-launch pre-order or a founding purchase is validation.

## 7\. Finding Conversations (Without Being Weird)

Cold outreach for "can I interview you" performs poorly and feels off-brand for a faceless luxury label. Better channels, in order of leverage:

1. **Go where they already gather.** Facebook groups, subreddits, TikTok comment sections, local meetups around the routine (haircare, home ambiance, kitchen). Participate genuinely; the conversations happen in-thread and in DMs.  
2. **Warm intros via the currency test.** Every good conversation ends with "who else should I talk to?" — an intro is both a reputation-currency commitment and the next conversation.  
3. **Create the gravity well.** Publishing useful content on the problem space (Herald's lane) makes the interested audience raise its own hand. Slower, compounding, on-brand.  
4. **Frame as advice, not sales.** People flee pitches and love being asked for expertise. "You clearly know this space — can I get your take?" opens doors that "check out my store" slams. Honest framing only: we genuinely are seeking their expertise. The moment it becomes a stealth pitch, it violates both the methodology and PHC ethics.

**PHC-specific proxy channels** (Augur's bread and butter, since a pre-launch dropshipper can't interview a customer base it doesn't have yet):

- **Ad Library comment mining:** comments under competitors' winning ads are unprompted, unsolicited voice-of-customer — the closest thing to clean field data that exists at zero cost. Apply the same taxonomy: discard compliments, harvest complaints, workarounds, and "I bought one and..." stories.  
- **Review corpus mining:** Amazon/AliExpress reviews on generic equivalents of PHC products. 1–3 star reviews \= problem inventory; 4–5 star reviews that mention a *prior* failed solution \= purchase-motivation gold.  
- **Post-purchase conversations:** once Founding 50 lands, every buyer gets a genuine two-question ask (see §9 scripts). This is where Vesta's future charter begins.

## 8\. Segment Until You Can Find Them

"Everyone who likes nice things" is not a customer segment — it's a fog. A segment is defined properly only when you can answer: *who are they specifically, and where do they consistently gather?* Slicing method: start broad, then subdivide by who wants it most, who's easiest to reach, and who will pay, until the group is findable.

- Bad: "women who buy beauty tools."  
- Workable: "US women 25–45 with thick or curly hair who follow haircare creators on TikTok and have already bought at least one hot tool that disappointed them."

The second version tells Augur where to research, tells Legatus how to target, and tells Scriptor which sophistication stage (Schwartz) the copy enters at. If a segment can't be located in a specific watering hole, it isn't a segment yet — keep slicing.

**Cross-reference:** segment choice interacts with the five-stage awareness model. Comment-mining a segment reveals its awareness stage empirically (are they problem-aware? solution-aware? burned by prior products \= high sophistication). This is the seam where Fitzpatrick's method feeds Schwartz's framework — conversations diagnose the stage; the stage dictates the copy.

## 9\. Running the Process (Discipline Layer)

The methodology fails silently when treated as vibes. Process rules:

1. **Prep: the Big Three.** Before any research sprint, write down the three things that would most change PHC's decisions if learned (e.g., "do buyers of curl tools care more about speed, damage, or hold?"). Every conversation/mining session aims at the Big Three. Update the list as questions get answered.  
2. **Seek the scary questions.** The most valuable question is the one with the power to kill the product. Asking only questions you'll like the answers to is validation-seeking wearing a research costume.  
3. **Notes verbatim, in their words.** Customer language is Scriptor's raw material. Paraphrasing at capture time destroys the asset. Capture exact phrases, tag by product/problem/emotion, store in the research corpus.  
4. **Review loop.** Findings reviewed against the Big Three after every sprint; contaminated data culled; surprises escalated to Ben. A finding that never changes a decision wasn't a finding.  
5. **Symptoms of doing it wrong:** every conversation feels great (you're pitching); notes are full of adjectives and empty of stories (you're collecting fluff); research keeps confirming existing plans (you're leading the witness); nobody ever says no (you never asked for commitment).

### Ready scripts (PHC register — direct, warm, zero corporate)

**Problem-discovery (pre-launch, in-community):**

- "What's the most annoying part of your \[hair routine / evening wind-down / kitchen cleanup\]?"  
- "When's the last time that happened? Walk me through it."  
- "What have you tried for it? How'd that go? What'd it run you?"

**Idea/feature deflection:** "Interesting — what would that fix for you? What are you doing about it right now?"

**Compliment deflection:** "Appreciate that — when did you last actually run into this?"

**Post-purchase (Founding 50):** "What almost stopped you from buying?" / "What were you using before, and what made you switch?" (Both past-tense, both specific, both feed directly into PDP objection-handling.)

**Commitment ask (late-stage):** "Founding batch is at cost because we need real reviews before launch — honest reason, honest deadline. Want in?"

## 10\. Ethics Reconciliation (PHC Filter)

The source methodology is already unusually clean — it's a framework for *hearing the truth*, which is PHC's whole brand thesis. Reconciliation notes:

- **Alignment:** no fabricated enthusiasm, no leading the witness, rejection treated as valuable — all native fits with the confession-as-flex register and the deleted-reviews origin story. This doctrine and the brand ethos are the same value expressed in research and copy respectively.  
- **Tightened (see §5):** disclosure-on-request is mandatory. Casual research never becomes covert research.  
- **Flag:** "advice framing" (§7.4) is permitted only while genuinely pre-sale with that person. Using an advice ask as a Trojan pitch is prohibited — it's manufactured social pretense, first cousin of manufactured social proof.  
- **Flag:** commitment asks must carry an honest reason-why (Founding 50 model). A commitment test with an artificial deadline would pass Fitzpatrick and fail PHC. PHC wins.
