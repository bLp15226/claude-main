## 1. Purchase-anxiety reduction on fitted goods

The strongest legitimate cluster in the source. All four items address the same
question: what does a buyer need to know before she will risk money on a garment she
cannot try on, from a store she has never heard of?

**1.1 The size chart, in inches, is the highest-value missing element.**
[16:00–17:28] Women's sizing is not standardised between makers — a size zero can be
XXS, XS or S depending on the brand — so the size *label* carries no fit information.
Buyers know their own measurements and use the chart to convert. State bust, waist and
hips, in inches for the US market. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

The speaker names the absence of a size chart on the teardown store as actively
suppressing its conversion rate, and attaches his own before/after: **~2.5% → ~3.0%
conversion rate** after adding one, a ~20% relative lift.
*STATED, PERFORMANCE, CLAIMED — provisional until PHC's own data confirms it.*

Method worth keeping independently of the number: he found the gap by auditing
competitors and noticing that **every** one of them carried a size chart. Convention
scanning across competitors as a way of finding a CRO gap you are blind to.

**1.2 The model reference line.** [17:29–18:04] State the model's height and the size
she is wearing — "she's 5'8, wearing a size extra small" — so the buyer can map the
photograph onto her own body and then adjust deliberately: same fit, buy that size;
looser, size up. Without it, every product photo is uncalibrated.
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**1.3 Returns stated plainly, adjacent to the buy button.** [08:34–09:07] For a first
purchase from an unknown store, a visible return promise is not a reassurance, it is a
**precondition of the sale** — "make sure that they know they can return the product or
else they won't even buy from your store in the first place." Placement matters: next
to the CTA, not buried on a policy page. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

> **RULED SPLIT — mechanic kept, intent rejected.** The speaker values this because
> ~90% of intending returners forget inside the window and end up keeping both items —
> "basically the perfect scenario." PHC keeps the placement rule and rejects the
> rationale outright. See §5.5. Revenue predicated on the customer failing to act is
> incompatible with "feel like family, not a transaction."

**1.4 On-body imagery.** [18:30–20:22] A dedicated block of real customers wearing the
garment, because the buyer needs drape and fit on a body, which a flat product shot
cannot supply. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.* **The source's execution of this is
rejected** — the images shown were of other brands' similar dresses, not the product.
See §5.4. The principle survives only with genuine customer imagery.

**1.5 Buyer-state model for this segment.** [07:53–08:15] Impulse purchase intent paired
with high scam-and-fulfilment skepticism — "they are impulse purchasers, but they are
extremely skeptical about the website." The two coexist, and the consequence is
structural: trust signals must sit **adjacent to the buy decision**, because the
impulse and the doubt arrive in the same moment. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**1.6 PHC synthesis (INFERENCE).** §1.1–1.4 are one mechanism, not four tactics:
each removes a specific *unresolved unknown* that blocks the purchase (will it fit /
what will it look like on me / what if I'm wrong / is this real). The generalisable
rule for PHC's own catalogue is to enumerate the unknowns a buyer cannot resolve from
a photograph, and answer each one on the page. This framing is ours, not the
speaker's.

## 2. Product-page architecture

**2.1 Above-the-fold priority order.** [06:16–06:48] Offer → product images → title →
price → benefits if space allows. Rationale: every ad click lands above the fold, so
the offer must be legible without scrolling. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**2.2 The offer is a stack, not a discount.** [10:52–11:22] The offer is not "80% off."
It is the discount **plus** free shipping with a named carrier **plus** secure checkout
**plus** the returns guarantee, working as one unit. The speaker flags this as his own
long-standing confusion — "your offer is all of those things combined."
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

> **CORROBORATION ONLY — no change proposed.** "Offer stack" is already carried in
> three Scriptor references (`PHC_Product_Page_Skeleton.md`, `PHC_Traffic_Doctrine.md`,
> `scriptor-doctrine-consumer-psychology.md`). Logged as a third-party independent
> arrival at existing doctrine, not as an addition. See proposal S-4.

**2.3 Product naming convention.** Proper first name + plain descriptor: "Marilyn —
Elegant Formal Slip Maxi Dress with Open Back"; "Sienna — Baby Blue Pleated Maxi Dress
with Sculptural Shell Corset." Frame-verified across three independent stores, so it
is a category convention rather than one operator's habit.
*STATED, PERSUASION, DEMONSTRATED.*

The proper noun does the memorability and the descriptor does the search and
comprehension work. Note the constraint this creates downstream: a named product is
findable by name, which is exactly the mechanism competitors use to source the
identical item (see the Augur file, §2).

**2.4 Us-versus-them comparison block.** [21:41–21:56] Present on the reference store,
frame-verified. *STATED, PERSUASION, DEMONSTRATED.*

> **RULED SPLIT — structure kept, copy rejected.** The block is a legitimate
> comparison device. The specific claims in it ("their dresses fit terrible," "theirs
> are scratchy") are invented. PHC may use the structure only with claims it can
> substantiate. See §5.6.

## 2A. Offer and AOV merchandising

> **ADDED 2026-07-29 by a second Aurelius ruling** splitting the original cart/checkout
> chunk. The offer/AOV merchandising half came here; the **customer-experience integrity
> half** (a broken "show more reviews" control, Shop Pay, mobile-first auditing, and the
> unverified Shopify-Plus checkout claim) is **still held** pending confirmation of which
> general owns customer-experience integrity. See the intake disposition record.

**2A.1 Set the free-shipping threshold above the hero product's price.** [05:36–05:49,
23:26–23:35] The threshold is not a shipping-cost recovery device, it is an **AOV
instrument**: placed above the price of the product the ads point at, it converts a
one-item purchase intent into a two-item basket. *STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS,
DEMONSTRATED.*

**2A.2 Catalogue depth exists to serve the threshold.** [05:01–05:35] The speaker runs
100+ listings with one or two hero products absorbing all traffic; the remaining catalogue
exists so the buyer has something to add in order to clear the threshold — "you need to
have a bunch of other products on your website so that they actually buy more stuff to get
the free shipping." *STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, ASSERTED.*

> **PHC synthesis (INFERENCE).** These two are one mechanism, and the order matters:
> a threshold without adjacent-desirable inventory is just a tax the buyer resents, and
> depth without a threshold is merchandising nobody navigates. The threshold creates the
> reason to browse; the catalogue makes browsing productive. Either alone underperforms.
>
> **PHC guardrail.** The gap must be *closable with something the buyer actually wants.*
> A threshold clearable only by padding the basket with items chosen for price rather than
> desire produces the purchase and the regret together — and regret on a first order is
> what a family-first brand can least afford.

**2A.3 State the gap explicitly in the cart drawer.** Frame-verified: on add-to-cart the
drawer surfaces the remaining distance to the threshold ("you're $70 away from free
shipping") rather than leaving the buyer to compute it. *STATED, PERSUASION,
DEMONSTRATED.* The mechanic is that an unstated threshold cannot motivate — the buyer has
to know the number and the distance at the moment the basket is in front of them.

**2A.4 Upsell placement.** Quick-add inside the cart drawer, and a second offer surfaced
in-checkout. *STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, DEMONSTRATED.* Recorded as placement
observation only — the teardown gives no performance data for either position, so nothing
here establishes which placement is worth having.

## 3. Creative complexity should scale to the product's explanation burden

**Ruled: Scriptor primary, light cross-note to Faber for creative-brief use.**

**3.1 The category test.** [04:15–04:50] For a visually self-evident impulse product,
the ad is the product photograph plus the price plus the offer. No mechanism, no
benefit stack. The speaker contrasts this explicitly with supplements, where the ad
must carry "this is magnesium, it'll help you sleep… here's why you need to buy it
right now" — and states that for dresses "all you basically have to do is show them
the product."
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.* Frame-verified at 04:18: the live ad is a single
full-body product photo, headline "20TH ANNIVERSARY SALE!", no body copy.
*DEMONSTRATED.*

**3.2 What disqualifies a product in a visual category.** Looks good + genuinely new +
not yet saturated across the internet. **Saturation, not quality, is the binding
constraint** — see the Augur file §3 on the copycat cascade.
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**3.3 Relationship to existing Scriptor doctrine — an extension, not a conflict.**
*(PHC synthesis, INFERENCE.)* This is sophistication-stage logic restated from the
operator's seat. A stage-1/stage-2 market responds to the plain claim and the product
itself; elaborate mechanism copy is what a *later*-stage market requires. Scriptor
already diagnoses awareness and sophistication stage before writing, and that
diagnosis remains the governing authority.

What the video adds is a **fast upstream heuristic** for the same call: *how much
does this product need explained before someone wants it?* A self-evident product
answered with heavy mechanism copy is wasted motion — and, more usefully, the reverse
error is the expensive one: treating a mechanism-dependent product as if showing it
were enough. Per Axis 1 the books govern the principle; the video contributes only
the shortcut.

## 5. ETHICS FILTER — REJECTED TACTICS (copy-side)

All 14 rejections approved by ruling 2026-07-29. Those in Scriptor's lane are recorded
here in full; they are **never** folded into the doctrine above.

**The headline finding:** the store the speaker holds up as "the golden formula for
high-converting women's clothing brand websites" is load-bearing on tactics PHC bans
by name. The page architecture in §2 survives; the persuasion layered onto it largely
does not.

**5.1 Fabricated reviews carrying "✓ Verified" badges. REJECTED — most serious item.**
Frame-verified at 22:55: named reviewers, five stars, customer photos, each marked
Verified. The speaker: "none of these customers bought this dress from this product.
None of them wrote these reviews. These are all completely fake." Two violations —
fabricated testimonials plus an affirmative false claim of verification.

> **One salvageable observation, and only as a rule for writing *real* testimonials:**
> he notes that short, specific, occasion-anchored reviews read as more credible than
> either one-word reviews or long Amazon-style paragraphs. That is a true observation
> about how proof reads. It applies to *soliciting and selecting genuine* customer
> language. It is not a licence to write any.

**5.2 False brand history. REJECTED.** "20th Anniversary Sale" on a store whose own
footer reads 2026. The speaker notices the impossibility and waves it off — "Anyways,
it doesn't matter. That's just part of e-commerce." A fabricated reason-why is exactly
what PHC's honest-reason-why rule prohibits.

**5.3 Fake scarcity and fabricated demand pressure. REJECTED.** "Only a few items
left," "only four dresses left in stock," "almost sold out," and "due to overwhelming
support during our 20th anniversary sale, only a few units remain at the current
price" — on **dropshipped goods the seller holds no inventory of**. Structurally false,
not merely unverified. Also rejected: the rolling "next 24 hours only… this sale ends
today" on a permanent evergreen discount, which the speaker praises as "hammering it
into the customer's head."

**5.4 Misrepresentative "as seen on" imagery. REJECTED.** The UGC block shows women in
visually similar dresses that are, by the speaker's own side-by-side of bodice
construction, not the garment being sold. He spends ninety seconds proving the
mismatch and treats it as normal.

**5.5 Returns framed as a forgetfulness bet. REJECTED (intent only).** See §1.3. Keep
the placement mechanic; reject the rationale.

**5.6 Unsubstantiated competitor disparagement. REJECTED (copy only).** See §2.4. Keep
the block; the claims must be true.

**5.7 Unsubstantiated price anchoring. REJECTED.** "$99, on sale from $200"; "up to 80%
off," with no evidence any unit sold at the anchor. PHC bans fake price anchoring by
name.

**5.8 Predetermined "scratch to win." REJECTED.** A game-of-chance frame on a discount
every visitor receives, gated behind email capture. Fake randomness.

**5.9 Engineered shipping-time ambiguity. REJECTED.** The speaker praises "USA 5 to 8
business days" precisely because business days convert to 14 calendar days while
*reading* shorter — "one way to make the shipping time seem lower but actually be a
little bit longer." Deliberate obfuscation of a material term. **And it does not even
reconcile:** the supplier cards frame-verified at 13:28 show 11–14 business days for
this garment, against a storefront promise of 5–8. The promise appears undeliverable
on its face, and the speaker does not notice.

**5.10 AI imagery passed off as product photography. REJECTED (practice).** Defended on
detectability — "normal people don't know what AI generated images look like."
Detectability is not the test; whether the image represents the product is. The
*artifact tells* were ruled salvageable as a QA checklist and routed to Faber.
