## 0\. INTAKE NOTES

**Source:** 1973 first edition, 158pp, two books in one volume. Book One \= "Dyna/Psyc" (goal-setting and self-conditioning system). Book Two \= the Direct Response business manual. All principles below are synthesized and restated — no verbatim reproduction.

**Historical position in the canon:** Karbo sits between Hopkins (scientific advertising — primary file pending; see classics §2.2) and Halbert, who openly idolized Karbo. **PHC synthesis (INFERENCE):** the claim that Halbert modeled the A-pile letter philosophy specifically on Karbo is our lineage inference — the admiration is documented, that specific modeling link is not. Karbo's own ad for this book — the famous "I used to work too hard" full-page — is one of the most profitable space ads ever run and is itself a masterclass in the confession register PHC already uses. **This is a load-bearing ancestor text for the PHC voice.**

**Reliability grading:**

- Book Two (business, pricing, copy) — **battle-tested doctrine.** Karbo spent his own money and kept records. Treat as peer to Hopkins.  
- Book One (Dyna/Psyc) — **founder psychology, not marketing doctrine.** Useful as operator discipline; its claimed mechanism ("psychic research," subconscious as literal computer) is 1970s pop-psych. Extract the practice, discard the metaphysics.  
- Media-buying chapters — **structurally obsolete** (rate cards, P.I. deals, split runs) but the underlying logic maps cleanly to Meta ads. Translated below rather than transcribed.

## 1\. THE ONE-SENTENCE KARBO

> Sell a real benefit to a real problem, in the plain voice of a friend writing a letter, at a price with room to advertise, test everything small, milk winners, kill losers fast, and treat the customer so well the reorder pays for the whole machine.

---

## 2\. BOOK TWO — DIRECT RESPONSE DOCTRINE (primary payload)

### 2.1 The economics: pricing law

**PHC synthesis (INFERENCE):** this is likely the origin of PHC's standing pricing rule — provenance inferred, not documented. Karbo states the rule itself harder than we currently practice it:

- **Absolute floor: sell at 3× total landed cost** (product \+ packaging/labor \+ shipping). Below 3× you have *no possibility* of profit — not "slim margin," none. Anything that can't clear 3× gets dropped, full stop: forget it and move to the next product.  
- **3× is a floor, not a target.** Karbo's comfort zone is 5×; at 10× the odds tilt decisively in your favor. Rationale: (a) margin is what lets you survive mistakes, and mistakes are guaranteed; (b) margin is what funds aggressive advertising, and advertising is the entire distribution arm.  
- **Budget 50% of revenue for customer acquisition.** Individual campaigns will range wildly (he saw 3% to 300%); 50% is the planning number every successful operator he knew converged on. PHC translation: expect blended CAC ≈ half of first-order revenue and build the P\&L to survive that.  
- **Higher price ≠ fewer dollars.** His worked example: half the units at a higher price can produce more profit with half the fulfillment workload — *unless* reorder economics reverse it. Lower entry price → more customers → more lifetime value. Which wins is an empirical question. **The public sets the price; you just ask them** (see testing, 2.4).  
- **The customer list is an appreciating asset.** Karbo turned down $1/name for a 75K list because renting it yearly earned more. PHC translation: the Omnisend list is a balance-sheet asset, not a byproduct. Every decision that grows or protects the list has hidden ROI.

### 2.2 The quantity-ladder ("3-5-7") — direct PHC application

Karbo's core cart-builder: base offer at X, double quantity at \~1.67X, triple at \~2.33X. Results from his real businesses: \~45/38/17 split across tiers, average order lifted well above base, and — the sleeper stat — **reorder average came in above the top tier**, because the ladder lets people *sample* cheaply and return convinced.

Rules for the ladder:

- **Quantity or size choices only. Never product choices.** Offering product A vs B vs C at the decision point confuses the buyer, and a confused buyer buys nothing. One solution per offer; the only question left is "how much."  
- This is precisely PHC's pre-purchase quantity-discount bump. Doctrine now has provenance and parameters: three tiers max, steepening per-unit discount, never a cross-sell at this stage. Cross-sells live post-purchase (thank-you page), which matches our standing architecture.

### 2.3 The 4 R's — Karbo's motivation model

Everything sold is bought for one of four reasons: **Legacy** (outliving yourself — works, children, monuments), **Recognition** (respect and admiration of others), **Romance** (desire, and after it, companionship and tenderness), **Reward** (material gain, status symbols, money).

Placement in the Scriptor stack: this is a coarser, punchier layer *under* Schwartz's awareness/sophistication model. Schwartz tells you *what the prospect already knows*; Karbo's 4 R's tell you *which nerve the promise must touch*. Every PHC headline should be traceable to one R. Beauty & Wellness → Romance/Recognition. Ambiance & Kitchen → Recognition ("my home says something about me"). Kids/Fur Babies → Legacy \+ Romance-as-tenderness.

### 2.4 Testing doctrine

- **Test one variable, simultaneously, on equivalent audiences.** His price test: identical letters, only price varies, same mail date, demographically balanced thirds. If the revenue difference between two prices is inside \~5%, take the *lower* price — more customers \= more list \= more reorders.  
- **An ad must stand on its own feet immediately.** In print, repetition does not rescue a loser; if the first run dies, the second does worse. (Broadcast is the exception — response *builds* with frequency because the audience needs multiple exposures to catch the offer. **PHC synthesis (INFERENCE):** that Meta ads behave like broadcast, not print — frequency has value up to fatigue — is our translation, not Karbo's claim.)  
- **Winners decay.** After a strong run, returns decline; rest the offer a month or two, then relaunch — often with a new headline. This is the ancestor of creative-fatigue rotation. Herald/Legatus: build "rest and re-headline" into the creative calendar rather than flogging a fatigued winner.  
- **Milk winners, cut losers, keep score.** His posting-sheet system (source, unit, key, cost, daily \+ cumulative orders/receipts, comments column for confounders like weather or bad placement) is the 1973 version of the Censor's dashboard. The comments column is the underrated part: log *why* a number might be off, or you'll learn false lessons.  
- **Test small before scaling.** A 2,000-piece test at 4% of the cost would have saved the guy who mailed 50,000 duds. Translation: minimum-viable ad spend before scaling any creative.

### 2.5 How to write the ad (Chapter Six — the crown jewel)

Karbo's opening instruction on how to write an ad is a single word: *don't.* Meaning: don't write "an ad." The moment copy sounds like advertising — stilted, clever, showing off vocabulary — it stops communicating. He calls the failure mode "Advertise-ese."

**The process:**

1. List everything the product *does* (never what it *is* — his wife didn't want a vacuum cleaner, she wanted a clean house with no effort).  
2. Pick the **one most important specific thing** and sharpen it until it's concrete: not "you can be important" but a promotion-and-raise promise with a number and a timeframe in it. Specificity is the drama.  
3. Saturate yourself with facts — every reason to buy, every objection — then *stop and do nothing*. The headline arrives unbidden. Then write in a flood; don't consult your notes until you're done (your judgment will have already filtered them).  
4. Cool the draft for two days. Read it aloud. **If it doesn't sound like you talking to one person, fix it. If you've coined something clever, kill it** — the moment readers notice style, they're entertained instead of persuaded.  
5. Show it to readers and ask exactly one question: **is it clear?** Watch their eyes; wherever they re-read, rewrite. Accept zero opinions on whether they *like* it, and never accept a "more refined" word swap. Clarity feedback yes, taste feedback no.

**Structure of every ad \= structure of a love letter.** Karbo demonstrates with an actual letter to his wife: headline that makes one person say "he's talking to me" → subhead that tells them they matter → the promise → advantages *and* disadvantages honestly enumerated → reassurance → identification of the seller → implied or explicit call to action. His second demo is a friend's misspelled note selling a Cadillac at half price: what it's selling, why it's cheap, how you can afford it, its one flaw, a free trial, and exactly what to do next. Atrocious grammar; irresistible offer. **A good product almost sells itself if you don't bury it under things people recognize as "advertising."**

**Length doctrine:** never decide length first. Write the most complete selling argument you can — every feature, every objection answered — then choose media to fit the copy, not copy to fit the media. Test the full-length version *first*; only then cut, using the RCA principle (build the best machine, then remove parts until just before it stops working), and let results — not taste — decide between long and short. "No one reads long copy" is true only of people who were never prospects; the person whose problem it is reads every word, and he's the only reader who counts.

**Admit the flaw.** Tell the disadvantages and the small weak points on purpose. It's the price of being believed, and belief is the whole game. (The Cadillac note's "awful green color" line is doing more selling than any superlative.) This is Karbo's version of the PHC confession register — see §4.

**The order point re-sells.** The coupon (for us: the cart, the checkout, the order-bump copy) restates the entire offer in shirt-sleeve English — what they get, what it does for them, the guarantee — because the buyer is signing a contract and knows it, and because by checkout they may have forgotten *why* they wanted it. Never let checkout copy go transactional-cold. Same rule inside the shipped package: include literature that re-sells the purchase, because the reorder decision starts the moment the box opens.

**Format tips that still translate (email/landing-page mapping):**

- No salutation gymnastics; open with the headline-as-first-sentence naming the problem you solve. The right reader self-selects.  
- Sentences \~10–12 words; paragraphs ≤5 sentences; indentation, underlining, sparing caps for visual rhythm — never whole sentences in caps (harder to read, reads as shouting/overselling — and overselling triggers "if it's so good, why is he pushing?").  
- Page-break cliffhangers: never let a section resolve at a natural stopping point; carry the reader across the fold. Email translation: the preview text and each section break should end mid-tension.  
- One person, one voice. Broadcast tip that generalizes: you're not addressing a stadium, you're telling one friend "hey — I've got something to tell you."  
- Repetition of ordering info is annoying to the uninterested and vital to the interested — and only the interested matter. Translation: repeating the CTA is fine; the non-buyer's mild irritation costs nothing.  
- Sound like a person, not an announcer. Stumbles add believability. (**PHC synthesis (INFERENCE):** the mapping to UGC-style creative beating polished studio creative is ours, that pattern itself is industry lore rather than a verified law, and the "Karbo predicted it by 50 years" framing is our flourish.)

### 2.6 Advertorial camouflage — ⚠️ handle with the ethics checklist

Karbo's strongest print tactic: make the ad indistinguishable from editorial — news layout, editorial typography, no reverse type, no design-y white space, illustrations only when words can't paint the picture. His don'ts (reverse printing, screened backgrounds, decorative white space) all reduce to one idea: **anything that signals "this is an ad" triggers learned distrust before the first word is read.**

**PHC ruling:** the *principle* (reduce ad-signal, increase substance-signal; native format; let the message carry the design) is sound and already implicit in our long-form advertorial format. The *literal tactic* (disguising paid content as independent editorial) fails both modern platform/FTC disclosure rules and the PHC honesty standard. We take "don't look like Advertise-ese" and leave "impersonate the newspaper." Scriptor's format-follows-destination protocol already handles this correctly; this entry exists so nobody re-imports the disguised version from the source text.

### 2.7 Customer-treatment doctrine (Vesta's inheritance)

Karbo is unusually forceful here and it reads like a PHC founding document:

- **Ship same day, next day at worst.** The mail-order buyer has sent money to a stranger for an unseen product and is nervous; speed is reassurance. If there's any delay, tell them *before* they ask.  
- **Answer every inquiry and complaint the day it arrives.** The man who threw away no-money envelopes was throwing away his best prospects: information-seekers are begging to be sold, and a well-handled complaint creates a loyal repeat buyer. His best-salesman anecdote: the guy who *asked* for the complaint desk outsold the whole firm.  
- **Call before they know.** When an order goes wrong, contact the customer proactively, apologize, ask how to make it right. Nine of ten will settle for less than you were prepared to give — because the caring is the product.  
- **Refund promptly and cheerfully.** He refunded so fast he occasionally refunded a bounced check — and the goodwill still netted out positive.  
- **Trust by default.** Ship without waiting for checks to clear; replace "never arrived" claims without demanding proof; don't punish all customers for the sins of a few. They trusted you first.  
- **Never embarrass a buyer over small money.** Underpaid ≤10%? Ship anyway. Billing someone over 50¢ tells them you caught them cheating — you risk the whole order and the relationship to collect coins.  
- The load-bearing sentence of the whole book: **no one gets rich except by enriching others** — and the enrichment must be real (easier, simpler, fuller life). He tells the miracle-seed con story specifically to note the con man ended up broke. Honesty isn't positioned as virtue; it's positioned as the only strategy that compounds, because **the big profit is in reorders, and only satisfied customers reorder.**

### 2.8 Product selection & ideation (Beowulf/Augur inheritance)

- **Best product \= unique: better, or cheaper, or a greater service** — ideally forcing the buyer to come to you.  
- **Inventory yourself first:** what do you enjoy and do better than average? (Better than half the population \= a market of a hundred million.) Then cross-combine two strengths for an unusual wedge.  
- **Creative Unhappiness:** keep a running list of what annoys you; every annoyance shared by millions is a product. **Turn worry into wampum:** solve your own biggest problem thoroughly, and the solution is a business (his debt book: solved his own $50K hole, then sold the method 100,000 times).  
- **Sell ideas over objects when possible** — "more people got rich selling cookbooks than running restaurants; paper is cheaper than plastic." PHC translation: content, guides, and information assets have infinite margin; consider info-adjacent upsells.  
- **Read the news as a want-list:** every headline is a mass worry, and every mass worry is a product brief. (Augur: this is a standing scan pattern, not a one-time exercise.)  
- **"What Would Happen If"** — systematic mutation of an existing product: bigger/smaller/inverted/cheaper material/pricier/different use. His shatterproof glow ornaments came from a failed anti-theft powder experiment; the door-viewer win came from asking why it only belonged in a front door.  
- **Don't overlook the obvious / don't accept the obvious.** Twin rules. The obvious answer everyone assumes "must have been tried" often hasn't (his first surplus deal). And the obvious industry consensus is often just follow-the-leader (see Zig-Zag, §4).  
- **Distributor warning:** never resell someone else's gadget without exclusivity — his coaster-chest story shows a winner being cloned down from $7.95 wood to $1.49 styrofoam. PHC translation: dropshipped commodity products have no moat except brand, positioning, and customer experience — which is exactly the PHC thesis. Also his warning against "buy-wholesale catalog" schemes: the parallel to low-effort dropshipping playbooks is exact, and it's the trap PHC's rebuild explicitly climbed out of.  
- **Milk winners; when you lay an egg, cut losses and move on.** You can afford many losers if tests are cheap; one winner pays for all of them. His own hit rate on ideas pursued "in white heat": \~50%. Ideas he second-guessed and reworked to hedge: 0%. **Conviction is a success factor; hedged, committee-ized ideas die.**

### 2.9 Media buying — translated to Meta (Legatus inheritance)

The mechanics are dead; the logic is alive:

- **Never pay rate card** \= never accept default/broad settings as given; the real price of attention is negotiated through structure (audiences, placements, bids).  
- **P.I. / pay-per-order deals** \= pure performance pricing. Modern equivalents: affiliate, commission-based creators, outcome-based partnerships. Karbo's caveat transfers exactly: don't *test* on performance deals — test on instrumented channels where you control measurement and can compare cleanly; use performance deals to scale proven offers.  
- **Position doctrine** (right-hand page, above the fold, first commercial in the break) \= placement and attention-quality matter enormously and platforms will show you "surveys" proving they don't. Trust your own records only.  
- **$2/thousand rule of thumb** \= he had a hard CPM ceiling derived from required ROAS (income ≥ 2× ad cost). The number is dead; the discipline — derive your max CPM/CPC backward from required return, and walk from anything above it — is the whole job.  
- **Fringe time / night people** \= underpriced attention pockets exist in every auction; loyal-audience contexts (talk, news, C\&W radio — "foreground" listening) outperform background contexts. Translation: attention quality \> audience size; buy where people actually attend.  
- **Response percentages are tiny and that's fine.** His funnel-attrition list (won't see → won't read → won't believe → won't act) is the original conversion-rate reality check. Success at a fraction of a percent is normal; anyone reasoning from "if only 2% of a million..." is an amateur.  
- **Seasonality:** Jan–May strong, summer weak, Oct–Nov rebound, Dec iffy — still directionally true for DR. Christmas offers: advertise Halloween→Nov 15, period.

## 3\. BOOK ONE — DYNA/PSYC (founder-discipline layer)

Strip the 1970s séance wrapping and Book One is a coherent operator OS. Extract for Ben's personal practice and for the Pedersen App Business section, not for customer-facing copy.

- **Goals must be written, or they don't exist.** The airport scene: you can't sell a ticket to "someplace nice." Fewer than 1-in-500 people have written, specific goals; those who do bend life to their terms.  
- **The nine-point goal checklist:** truly wanted (not respectable-sounding); mutually consistent (a $250K house goal contradicts a $20K income goal — raise the income goal); family-aligned; stated positively (what you want, never what you're escaping); *detailed to the point of a purchase order*; realistic only in the sense that some human could do it; **set at the ceiling of desire, because a timid goal is a self-installed ceiling** — the goal is a floor, not a ceiling; includes the character traits achievement will require; and **worded as already accomplished** ("I own / I am," never "I want / I wish").  
- **How is not your problem at goal-setting time.** Deciding achievability while listing wants is the failure mode. Separate the wanting from the planning.  
- **Daily Declarations:** read the full goal list aloud (or lip-moved) on waking and before sleep, visualizing each concretely — touch the steering wheel, count the bills. Two non-negotiable sessions; consistency is the entire mechanism; missing days collapses the effect.  
- **Super Suggestion:** deep-relaxation self-suggestion, one character trait at a time, keyed to a code word — **intangibles only.** His warning is psychologically sharp: convince the deep mind you already *have* the object and it stops helping you get it; convince it you already *are* the trait and you start acting like it, which is the goal. Behave-as-if works for character, backfires for possessions.  
- **The Unconscious Computer (Write–Try–Ask):** write the problem out fully ("half of problems exist only because they were never defined"), exhaust conscious analysis with a pro/con sheet, then formally assign it to the back of your mind with a deadline — and *forget it*. The answer arrives with a felt sense of rightness and eagerness. Two riders: honest deadlines only (need drives speed), and **act on every answer you're given or the channel closes.** (Mechanistically this is just incubation \+ diffuse-mode cognition; it's real, and Karbo's protocol for invoking it deliberately is better than most modern productivity writing.)  
- **Silent Treatment:** one daily 5–20 minute eyes-closed do-nothing reset. One per day only.  
- **Secrecy rule:** tell no one your goals except a co-practitioner. Two stated reasons that hold up: early conviction is infection-prone, and *announcing substitutes for achieving* — telling the story discharges the energy that should have gone into the work. (Modern research on identity-goal announcement agrees.)  
- **ISI (Inadequate Self-Image):** everyone's ceiling is a self-image set in early childhood by fear-conditioning; the average person runs at \~10% of capacity because of it. Two killer illustrations: the Ford salesmen who each earned, month after month, exactly what they *expected* — not what they wished; and the real-estate ad that failed at "$50,000/year" and flooded at "$20,000/year" because applicants couldn't *believe* the bigger number about themselves. **People respond to the promise they can believe they deserve** — which is also a copywriting law: calibrate the promise to the prospect's self-image, not to the product's actual ceiling.  
- **Time math:** Declarations (20 min) \+ Super Suggestion (10) \+ one focused hour \= \~9% of waking hours, reclaimed from TV and worry. 9% of a life is seven years.  
- **The regret principle** (his father-in-law): the worst case of a bold move is a good memory; the only regrets are the things not done. Paired with: when you've chosen, jump — a two-foot chasm is only dangerous to a timid step.  
- **Failure taxonomy:** (1) clear goals, ineffective pursuit; (2) furious effort, no rudder; (3) neither. Diagnose which one you are before prescribing.  
- **Everyone is a salesman; the good ones earn more because they expect more.** The 15-calls-per-sale real-estate math ($56.66 per call *including the no's*) is the original "every no has a dollar value" reframe — useful for cold outreach and ad-test psychology alike: a failed creative test is a paid-for step toward the winner.

**Pedersen App hook:** Daily Declarations \+ goal checklist \+ Write-Try-Ask are directly implementable in the Business section (Aurelius dashboard) — a morning/evening declarations surface tied to the $10K/month milestone tracker would be Karbo running on Supabase.

## 4\. KARBO ↔ PHC VOICE: THE CONFESSION LINEAGE

This is the most important strategic finding of the extraction. Karbo's Ford-agency campaign is PHC's origin story run 50 years early: a small dealer, inconvenient location, green salesmen — and the winning campaign *said exactly that*, plus "we're in business to make a profit, at least enough to still be here when you need service." No fake bargains, live unedited customer interviews, distrust of too-cheap prices made explicit. Result: the agency netted a million dollars in three years while the zigging competitors went broke.

His formal name for the move: **Operation Zig-Zag** — map what every competitor does, then test the exact opposite. And his summary line for it: *when all else fails, tell the truth; it works wonders in advertising.*

Doctrine consequences:

- PHC's "confession as flex, not apology" register is not a novelty bet; it is a proven direct-response strategy with a documented seven-figure precedent, from an author whose own signature ad was a confession ("I was $50,000 in debt...").  
- The believability mechanics generalize: admit the flaw (§2.5), show honest reason-why for every price and promise, never oversell (overselling reads as desperation), and let trust do the closing — **you have to be trusted to sell once, and live up to it to sell twice.**  
- Karbo's honest-reason-why for his own book price ("$10 × 100,000 buyers \= I make a million doing what I love") is a template: PHC can state its margins' purpose (funding the brand, the service, the guarantee) as a trust asset rather than hiding them.

## 5\. ETHICS & OPTIMIZER-DRIFT FLAGS (standing-rules audit)

Per the Scriptor optimizer-drift checklist, items in the source that must **not** enter PHC practice unmodified:

1. **Advertorial disguise** (§2.6) — adopt the anti-Advertise-ese principle, reject editorial impersonation. Modern disclosure rules and PHC honesty policy both bar it.  
2. **Under-the-table media discounts / back-dated contracts** — period artifacts; the surviving principle is "never pay sticker for attention," pursued through legitimate structure.  
3. **Income-claim advertising style.** Karbo's own ad leans on personal income/lifestyle claims. They were true and he offered proof, but the *pattern* is the ancestor of the fake-guru genre Ben has been burned by. PHC does not trade in income or outcome claims it cannot substantiate per-customer.  
4. **"It's worked, without exception, for everyone who's tried it"** — Book One contains unfalsifiable universal claims. Noted as the register PHC never uses: our confession voice earns trust precisely by refusing this move.  
5. **Sales-tax / bookkeeping chapters** — 1973 California; historically interesting, operationally void. Modern equivalents are handled by Shopify/Quaestor.

Everything else in the extraction is compatible with the standing rules (no fake urgency, no fabricated testimonials, no manufactured social proof, honest reason-why). Notably, Karbo *independently derives* most of the standing rules from pure profit motive — useful ammunition when external feedback (ChatGPT or otherwise) claims honesty tactics "leave money on the table."

## 7\. TWENTY LINES FOR THE WALL (compressed doctrine)

1. 3× landed cost or forget it; 5× to breathe; 10× to win.  
2. Plan for half of every first dollar going to acquisition.  
3. The list is the asset; the product is how you build the list.  
4. Quantity ladders, never product ladders, at the point of decision.  
5. Every purchase is Legacy, Recognition, Romance, or Reward — name the R before writing the headline.  
6. Sell what it *does*; nobody wants the vacuum cleaner.  
7. One specific promise beats ten vague ones.  
8. Write the ad as a letter to one person who has the problem.  
9. If it sounds clever, kill it; if it sounds like you, keep it.  
10. Ask readers only "is it clear?" — never "do you like it?"  
11. Write it all, test it long, then cut until just before it breaks.  
12. Admit the flaw; it's the price of being believed.  
13. Re-sell at the checkout and inside the box; the reorder starts there.  
14. Nothing that signals "ad" — and nothing that hides that it is one.  
15. Repetition never rescues a dead ad; rest and re-headline the tired winner.  
16. Test small, one variable, simultaneously; let records overrule taste.  
17. Ship same day; answer complaints first; refund cheerfully; trust by default.  
18. No one gets rich except by enriching others — the reorder proves it.  
19. When they zig, zag; when all else fails, tell the truth.  
20. Written goals, stated as done, declared daily, told to no one, worked in white heat.
