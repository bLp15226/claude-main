## 1 — The framework

Per the source, effective e-commerce ads do four things, in order:

1. **Call out an avatar.** The viewer registers that the ad is addressed to them specifically.
2. **Educate that avatar on the problem.** Move them to recognising it as a real problem, not an
   ambient annoyance.
3. **Position the product's mechanism as the *unique* solution** to that problem. He stresses
   *unique* — the mechanism, not the product category, is what does the work.
4. **Deliver an offer** — a value exchange that is urgent and limits risk, so the purchase happens
   now rather than later.

*STATED · PERSUASION PRINCIPLE · ASSERTED.* No data offered; presented as how the speaker's agency
approaches creative across its client base.

## 2 — How this sits against Scriptor's existing doctrine

**Steps 1–3 are orthodox and largely already covered.** The problem → mechanism → unique-solution
progression is standard direct response and overlaps material already in the Scriptor stack. The
framework's contribution is not novelty but **compression**: four ordered jobs, checkable against
a draft in seconds.

**Step 1's emphasis is the part worth carrying across.** Under the platform behaviour already
adopted in Faber v1.1.0, calling out an avatar is not only a persuasion device — it is what
determines *delivery*. That makes step 1 load-bearing in a way a purely editorial reading of the
framework would miss: a draft that opens without a clear addressee is a targeting problem before
it is a copy problem. Cross-reference, not a new claim — the mechanism was adjudicated from a
prior Piliero source.

**Step 3's "unique" is the most demanding element in practice.** It requires PHC to be able to
state what its product does that alternatives do not — which is an Augur question (mechanism,
differentiation) before it is a Scriptor one. A draft cannot manufacture a unique mechanism that
research has not established.

## 3 — ⚠ CONDITIONAL ADOPTION — step 4's "urgent"

> **Ruled 2026-07-28: adopt the framework only with PHC's honest-reason-why constraint attached.
> Logged as a conditional, not a blanket adoption.**

**What the source does and does not say.** He calls for an offer that is urgent and limits risk. He
**never suggests manufacturing** the urgency — no invented deadlines, no fake countdown, no
fabricated scarcity. On its own terms the claim does not trip PHC's fake-urgency rule, and the
ethics filter on this source returned nothing rejected.

**Why it still needs the constraint.** Adopted verbatim, step 4 states a requirement ("be urgent")
without stating where the urgency may come from. That is precisely the gap through which a
fabricated deadline enters — not by intent, but because the instruction creates a demand the writer
must satisfy and does not say what satisfies it legitimately.

**The constraint, as it applies here:**

> Urgency in step 4 must rest on a **real reason-why** — a genuine deadline, a genuine stock
> position, a genuine seasonal or circumstantial fact. If no honest reason for urgency exists, step
> 4 is satisfied by the **risk-limiting** half alone (guarantee, returns, trial terms) and the
> urgency element is dropped. **A missing reason-why is never manufactured to complete the
> framework.**

This is consistent with Scriptor's existing banned-tactics list (fake scarcity, manipulative
urgency, fake price anchoring) — the framework does not override it, and where the two meet, the
banned-tactics list governs.

**Precedent note.** This is the same shape as the Blackie mortality-framing assessment
(2026-07-28): urgency grounded in something genuinely true passes PHC's filter; urgency asserted
because the format calls for it does not.
