## 0. THE PRIORITY FINDING — REASON-WHY IS NOT HERE

This extraction was commissioned to settle whether the classics §2.2 rule — *every
offer, discount, or deadline needs an honest stated reason* — originates in this book.

**Verdict: it does not. STATED-absence, checked exhaustively:** the phrase "reason why"
occurs four times in the full text, every one incidental prose ("the reason why men
could not use…"), never as doctrine. No chapter, including "Reasons for Success" and
"Scientific Advertising," states any rule about justifying offers or deadlines. The
same absence holds in the 1923 book per its primary report.

What the book *does* contain is the **lineage, not the law**:

- **The Powers stories (ch. 4) — STATED as anecdote.** John E. Powers, whom young
  Hopkins idolized, saved a Pittsburgh clothier by announcing the true reason for the
  sale (impending bankruptcy) and sold out a stock of mackintoshes by printing the
  buyer's own private admission that they were damaged goods, priced accordingly.
  Honest-reason-for-the-deal, demonstrated spectacularly — attributed by Hopkins to
  Powers, and never generalized by Hopkins into a rule.
- **"Tell the reasons" (Schlitz, ch. 7) — STATED, but it's a different rule.** Hopkins's
  own reason-giving doctrine is about *substantiating claims* (tell the facts behind
  "pure" instead of shouting "pure" louder), i.e. reason-why-*copy* in the old Lord &
  Thomas sense — not about justifying deals.
- **INFERENCE (PHC synthesis, external history):** "reason-why advertising" as a school
  is usually credited to John E. Kennedy at Lord & Thomas (~1904), where Hopkins later
  worked. Plausible provenance for the *term*; not evidence Hopkins taught the
  deal-justification rule.

**Consequence, executed 2026-07-23:** the PHC deal-honesty rule is re-grounded on
Collier (the disclosed-damage bargain-news pattern, primary in folder), Karbo (the
stated purpose behind his own book's price, primary in folder), and Sugarman (§8,
honest reason-why for price), with Powers-via-Hopkins as illustrative lineage. The
rule itself is unchanged — it was always right; only its citation was wrong.

## 1. THE ONE-SENTENCE BOOK

> Offer service instead of asking for a purchase, take the risk on yourself, tell the
> specific ordinary facts your rivals think too commonplace to mention, trace every
> result to the penny, and let the customer's actual response — never anyone's
> judgment, including yours — decide everything.

## 2. DOCTRINE BY THEME

### 2.1 Service-first selling — the ask-nothing architecture (STATED, the book's spine)

Hopkins's entire career restates one move: never ask people to buy; offer them the
favorable end of a transaction and let reciprocity and self-interest close.

- The Bissell campaigns sold record volumes of sweepers *to dealers* by offering a
  privilege with conditions (display racks, signed agreements, limited allocations of
  novel woods) — "I appeared as a benefactor, not a salesman," with the dealer
  soliciting him. PHC translation: structure offers so accepting them is obviously in
  the buyer's interest, and let the conditions (real ones) do the qualifying.
- The Liquozone resurrection: buy the customer's first bottle outright, then guarantee
  the six-bottle course through the local druggist the customer already trusts. A
  bankrupt company to $1.8M net in the first fiscal year. The mechanism: **make an
  offer no reasonable person can refuse, with the seller carrying the risk** — and his
  operating observation that when you visibly trust people, claim rates stay tiny
  (under ~2% on the guarantee), because trust invites people to justify it.
- **Guarantee grammar (STATED):** the druggist-signed warrant worked because the
  guarantor was a known neighbor, not a distant stranger. Modern mapping: the
  guarantee gains force from *who visibly stands behind it* and how concrete the
  claim process is — feeds the classics §4.6 forward-trust framing.

### 2.2 "We Will Buy" beats "Free" — the sampling doctrine, with teeth (STATED)

The most operational nuance in the book, and it sharpens the stack's existing
risk-reversal doctrine three ways:

1. **Never sample the uninterested.** Promiscuous doorstep sampling failed everywhere
   he measured it (Puffed Grains, Van Camp). A sample only converts after the story
   has created desire — so the coupon comes at the *end* of the argument, and the
   reader must take an action to claim it.
2. **"We will buy you one" out-pulls "free."** Paying the retailer full price for the
   customer's trial package (Palmolive, Van Camp Milk) reads as confidence; "free"
   reads as cheapness. Same economics, opposite signal. Bonus mechanic: the announced
   coupon forces universal retail stocking with zero salesmen, because every grocer
   knows the coupon is as good as cash.
3. **Category caveat — the hygiene exception (STATED, Pepsodent):** leading with
   "FREE" worked for foods but *quartered* results for hygiene/importance-positioned
   products — the gift framing turned a scientific benefactor into a peddler. The
   trial stayed free; the *featuring* of free-ness was the poison. PHC translation:
   for wellness/beauty positioning, lead with the benefit and the test, never with
   the giveaway; and never charge for a trial to "qualify" buyers — his math showed a
   10¢ sample fee multiplying acquisition cost fivefold.

### 2.3 The commonplace-facts engine — origin of the mechanism block (STATED)

This book contains the origin stories behind the stack's preemptive-claim doctrine,
and they sharpen its wording:

- **Schlitz:** every brewer cried "pure"; Hopkins toured the brewery, and printed the
  ordinary industry-standard facts — plate-glass cooling rooms, 4,000-foot wells,
  bottles washed four times, the 1,200-experiment mother yeast cell — that every rival
  could have claimed and none had. Fifth place to contention with first, and his own
  stated generalization: being *first to tell* commonplace facts gives lasting,
  exclusive prestige, and if rivals repeat them afterward they only advertise you.
- **Van Camp:** identical play on pork and beans (nobody could tell the brands apart
  blind), plus attacking the real competitor — home baking, the 94% — rather than the
  6% who already bought canned. And "Try Our Rivals, Too" as the confidence flex.
- **Palmolive Shaving Cream — the full recipe (STATED), new to the stack in this
  explicit form:** (1) interview users by the hundreds for what they *want*;
  (2) take those wants to the maker's chemist for *measured figures* (lather
  multiplied 250×, full cream held ten minutes, beard 15% water-absorbed in one
  minute); (3) publish the figures. Market dominance in eighteen months, in a
  category where he conceded rivals could probably match the specs. **This is the
  PHC mechanism-block pipeline stated as procedure: Augur asks, Centurion verifies,
  Scriptor prints the numbers.** The claim wins not because the product is unique but
  because the *measurement* is unprecedented.
- Wording correction this supplies to classics §2.2: the move is *say the true
  ordinary thing first* — not "state it as if unique to you." He never counsels
  implying uniqueness; the first-teller owns the fact without any as-if.

### 2.4 Specificity — definite figures are accepted at par (STATED)

Same law as the 1923 book, with this book's own artillery: "Our Profit is 9 Per Cent"
(Chalmers, against the profiteering suspicion), the 3%-net-profit campaign that beat
six rivals' shouting-match bargain claims, tungsten's "3⅓ times the light" vs. "more
light." His mechanism sentence: platitudes are *expected* and discounted, while a
definite figure forces the reader to choose between "fact" and "they're lying in
print" — and readers extend the benefit of the doubt to specifics. PHC rider stands:
that credit is exactly why unverified precision is fraud (see the standing
25%-close/founder-photo lessons).

### 2.5 Traced results — the epistemology, from age nine (STATED)

- His first traced test was distributing bills as a boy: one bill per home, every home
  covered, results compared against the cheaper boys who dumped and skipped — and a
  monopoly followed. "Known and compared returns" as a lifetime religion from there:
  keyed coupons on everything, headline variants compared weekly (Pepsodent), whole
  strategies reversed inside a week when returns said so.
- **The Pinkerton lesson (STATED):** the mayor threw his beloved detective book on the
  floor; the store manager bought seven. Never judge the market by yourself or any
  board of directors — "submit all things to the court of public opinion." His odds:
  a guess is right maybe one time in fifty; a test is right fifty in fifty.
- **The consumer-vote test (STATED, Quaker):** Two-minute Oats died because tested
  users disliked the flavor; Quick Quaker won ~90% of a mailed preference vote before
  rollout. Cheap polls of *actual users* on *actual product decisions* — distinct
  from surveys of stated intent, which his beer data (via the canon) says to distrust.
  The distinction matters: ask users to compare experiences, never to predict
  behavior.
- Mail-order as calibration: he kept mail-order accounts all his life *because* their
  merciless economics ("42 cents vs. $14.20 per reply" for plain vs. pretty) kept his
  feet on the ground. Ancestor of the stack's rule that ad-account data replaces book
  benchmarks within weeks.

### 2.6 Curiosity, personality, and the crowd (STATED, with one ethics gate)

- **Curiosity:** "foods shot from guns," the vermilion-wood rajah story, the secret
  gift (undisclosed present for six labels) — his stated view that no activating
  factor compares with curiosity, PLUS the honesty rider he states himself: the
  secret gift must be *better* than the buyer was led to expect. Over-deliver on
  mystery or don't use it.
- **Featured personality:** Professor Anderson (Puffed Grains), Howard Coffin and the
  48 named engineers (Chalmers/Hudson), R. E. Olds signing "My Farewell Car," Edna
  Wallace Hopper's name carrying an entire cosmetics line. People bond with a person,
  not a corporation; making a person famous makes the product famous. Direct ancestor
  of the Attractive Character; consistent with PHC's brand-as-character approach.
  ⚠ One instance crosses the line — see §3.
- **The crowd-trend lever:** the Overland tent-city romance, Goodyear's publicized
  growth curve, "how many men bought Van Camp's downtown today" — people follow
  visible demand because they can't judge value alone. **Ethics gate:** ships at PHC
  only with real, verifiable numbers (a true growth fact, a true customer count);
  manufactured momentum is the banned twin (SKILL.md constraint 1/2 territory).

### 2.7 Where not to spend (STATED — the restraint doctrine)

- **Never pay to change habits.** His costliest lesson: the Quaker Oats education
  campaign to convert non-oatmeal-eaters could never repay conversion cost (his
  figure: a converted tooth-brush user cost ~$25 against decades of paste margin).
  Advertise to people already schooled in the habit; ride trends, never create them.
- **Cure over prevention; rewards over penalties.** Pepsodent won as a *beauty*
  product (the film, the smile) after he explicitly rejected both the preventive
  frame and the scare frame — his summary: people will do anything to fix a felt
  trouble and almost nothing to avoid a hypothetical one, and repulsive imagery
  loses readers. Confirms the bright-house rule with its strongest case study.
- **Sell the consumer once, not twice.** Winning the consumer *and* bribing the
  trade (free goods, extra margins, salesman armies) is the classic advertising
  bankruptcy; consumer demand pulls distribution by itself (Palmolive's $100K+ of
  advance orders from dealers who'd never seen the soap). Maps to PHC's DTC reality
  more than to marketplaces, but the principle — don't pay twice for the same sale —
  generalizes to affiliate/discount stacking.
- **Frivolity fails.** Spending money is serious to people who have little; humor
  and cleverness entertain without converting ("people do not buy from clowns" is
  his phrasing — same law as write-for-money-not-applause, third source).

### 2.8 Operator layer — for Ben, not for copy

- **Industry over brains (STATED):** his opening claim — the difference is hours and
  reps, not talent; the man who does 2–3× the work learns 2–3× as fast, from both
  mistakes and wins. And: "no mistake twice."
- **Graduate from the expense class (STATED):** his bookkeeper epiphany — expenses
  get minimized, profit-earners command a share of what they create. The Boron/Karbo
  self-reliance thread, stated as career math.
- **"My Great Mistake" (STATED):** staying salaried for 35 years instead of taking
  Bissell's advice to own his own enterprises; commissions made him comfortable while
  equity in the businesses he built (Pepsodent stock: $13K in, ~$700K out) made him
  rich. PHC translation: Ben already owns the enterprise — the inherited discipline
  is the inverse: pay for growth from traced results, never from hope.
- **Conviction against consensus (STATED):** every major upward move he made was
  unanimously opposed by friends; the average adviser is average. Rhymes with Karbo's
  white-heat rule and Boron's don't-announce rule — third source, so now settled
  doctrine for the operator layer.

## 3. ETHICS SCREEN — what stays in 1927

Hopkins is mostly clean (truth-and-specifics is his whole engine), but four items get
formally rejected and one gets a caution:

1. **The undisclosed second brand (ch. 7 — REJECTED).** Racine Incubators (premium)
   and Belle City (budget) were secretly the same operation; price-objectors were
   "lost" to a competitor that was actually the house. Modern equivalent — running a
   hidden budget brand to catch your own refusers without disclosure — fails PHC
   honesty outright. (Disclosed multi-brand portfolios are a different, legitimate
   thing.)
2. **The ghost-authored "independent expert" (ch. 7 — REJECTED as framed).** The
   incubator book was written by Hopkins in a real chicken-raiser's name, with his
   facts and consent — but marketed as an independent practical man's disinterested
   advice. Consent doesn't cure the misrepresentation of independence; this is
   advertorial camouflage's cousin, already banned (Karbo §2.6 ruling, SKILL.md
   constraint 7).
3. **The fictional signing persona (ch. 15 — REJECTED).** The installment-garment
   business fronted by a constructed woman who "wrote" personal credit letters to
   customers — a fabricated person presented as a real one, at scale. The mechanism
   (one-person voice, credit-as-flattery framing, cross-line trust referrals between
   sister companies) is brilliant and instructive; the fabrication is the part PHC
   never imports. Note the collier.md §9 ruling already covers this class; Hopkins
   supplies its most seductive specimen.
4. **Medical advertising (chs. 7–8 — REJECTED by Hopkins himself).** He renounces the
   category in his own text ("a class of advertising of which I no longer approve,"
   refusing $900K to do it again) — a rare primary-source case of an author
   ethics-correcting his own career. Useful precedent: PHC wellness copy stays
   sensory/appearance, never treatment claims — the standing rule, now with Hopkins's
   own signature under it.
5. **Caution — the Mitchell lesson (STATED, keep):** his most *effective* campaign
   destroyed the client because the product couldn't cash the advertising's check —
   "we played too high a note for the product we had to sell." Great copy on a bad
   product accelerates the funeral. This is the Founder's First Order rule, argued
   from the seller's self-interest.
