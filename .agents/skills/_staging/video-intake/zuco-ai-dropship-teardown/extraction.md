## 1. Creative complexity should scale to the product's explanation burden

**1.1 The category test.** [04:15–04:50] For a visually self-evident impulse product,
the ad is the product photograph plus price plus offer — no mechanism, no benefit
stack. The speaker contrasts this explicitly with supplements, where the ad must
carry "this is magnesium, it'll help you sleep, here's why you need it." *STATED,
PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

Frame-verified at 04:18: the live Pinterest ad is a single full-body product photo,
headline "20TH ANNIVERSARY SALE!", byline "Barbara Dever Boutique", Sponsored. No
body copy visible. *DEMONSTRATED.*

**1.2 What makes a winner in a visual category.** Looks good + genuinely new + not
yet saturated across the internet. Saturation, not quality, is the disqualifier.
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**1.3 PHC synthesis (INFERENCE, not stated).** This is a restatement of
sophistication-stage logic from the book stack, not a contradiction of it: a
stage-1/stage-2 market responds to the plain claim and the product itself; elaborate
mechanism copy is what a *later*-stage market requires. Scriptor already diagnoses
awareness and sophistication stage before writing. The video's contribution is a
usable **fast category heuristic** — how much explanation does this product need
before someone wants it — that sits upstream of the stage diagnosis, and a warning
that copy volume applied to a self-evident product is wasted motion. Flagging as an
extension to existing doctrine, not new doctrine and not a conflict.

## 3. Purchase-anxiety reduction for fitted apparel

This is the strongest legitimate cluster in the video.

**3.1 The size chart, in inches.** [16:00–17:28] Women's sizing is not standardised
across brands — a size zero can be XXS, XS or S depending on the maker — so the buyer
cannot infer fit from the label. Buyers know their own measurements and use the chart
to convert. The teardown store has **no size chart**, which the speaker names as
actively suppressing its conversion rate. Use inches for the US market.
*STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.* Frame-verified at 16:03 that the reference store has
one. **Attached performance claim:** adding a size chart moved his own conversion
rate from ~2.5% to ~3.0% — a ~20% relative lift. *STATED, PERFORMANCE, CLAIMED.*

Method note worth keeping: he found this by auditing competitors and noticing every
one of them had a size chart. Competitor-convention scanning as a CRO gap-finder.

**3.2 Model reference line.** [17:29–18:04] State the model's height and the size she
is wearing ("5'8, wearing XS") so the buyer can map the photograph onto her own body
and adjust up or down deliberately. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

**3.3 Returns stated plainly, near the buy button.** [08:34–09:07] For a first purchase
from an unknown store, a visible 30-day return promise is a **precondition of the
sale** — "make sure they know they can return the product or else they won't even buy
from your store in the first place." *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.*

> **Split ruling required.** The *mechanic* (state returns clearly, adjacent to the
> CTA) is sound and PHC-compatible. The speaker's *reasoning* is not: he values it
> because ~90% of intending returners forget inside the window and are then stuck
> with both items — "basically the perfect scenario." See §7.13. Keep the mechanic,
> reject the intent.

**3.4 On-body customer imagery.** [18:30–20:22] A dedicated section of real customers
wearing the garment, because buyers need to see drape and fit rather than a flat
product shot. *STATED, PERSUASION, ASSERTED.* **The reference store's execution of
this is rejected** — see §7.7.

**4.2 Catalogue depth serves the threshold, not browsing.** ~100+ products live, one

*STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, ASSERTED.*

**4.3 Build once, rotate product tests.** [05:01–05:19] A good-looking store plus a deep

**4.5 Shop Pay on by default.** [23:44–23:55] Leaving Shop Pay enabled produces

**4.6 Post-add and in-checkout upsells.** Cart-drawer quick-add, shipping insurance,

## 5. Paid-media pattern

**5.1 The claimed launch curve.** [01:31–02:26] TikTok, from a standing start on
April 1: day 1 $100 spend → $271 sales; day 2 duplicated the campaign, $200 → $460;
day 3 added fresh creatives and **raised budgets on the first two campaigns** → $835;
day 4 duplicated the winners into a new campaign, ~$600 → $1,471. Thereafter
"consistent," and the job becomes continuous creative production.
*STATED, PERFORMANCE, CLAIMED* (numbers narrated over a dashboard; not legible in
frames) / *PLATFORM MECHANICS, ASSERTED* (the mechanic itself).

**5.2 Creative volume is the scaling lever.** Once a product works, the operating loop
is making new creatives and launching them. *STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, ASSERTED.*
Consistent with Faber's flywheel premise.

**5.3 Creative sourcing by product name.** [26:34–26:55] Search "<product name> TikTok"
to surface existing organic videos of the same physical garment, and use those as raw
material for your own creatives. Frame-verified: a results grid of many videos of the
same dress. *STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, DEMONSTRATED.*

> **Rights caveat, PHC synthesis (INFERENCE).** He does not address permission.
> Using someone else's video as ad creative without a licence is a rights problem
> regardless of how easy it is. If this route is adopted at all it needs a
> licensing/permission step attached, which the video does not supply.

**5.4 CONFLICT WITH LEGATUS — verified, and narrower than it first appears.**

Checked against `legatus-meta-ads/references/diagnostic-taxonomy.md` and
`legatus-video-blackie-7day-dropship.md`:

- **Not a conflict:** duplicating rather than editing. Legatus already prescribes
  exactly this — "duplicate into a new ad set instead of editing" (taxonomy L77), and
  "duplicate the account's best performers into a new ad set" for isolated tests.
  Grant's day-2 and day-4 duplications are doctrinally aligned.
- **Real conflict:** the **day-3 budget increase on two-day-old campaigns.** Legatus's
  checklist states "Day 1-3: no edits to a newly launched ad set (avoid re-triggering
  learning phase)" (taxonomy L381), and the already-ingested Blackie source is filed
  as explicitly changing nothing on day 1. Grant raises budgets on day 3 on campaigns
  launched days 1–2.
- **A qualifier that prevents clean adjudication:** Grant's account is **TikTok**;
  Legatus is Meta-specific and is built on Meta's learning phase. Whether TikTok's
  delivery system penalises a day-3 budget edit the same way is **not established by
  either source.** Treating this as a doctrine conflict may be a category error —
  it could equally be two correct rules for two different platforms.

**Do not resolve this here.** The honest state: aligned on duplication, contradictory
on the edit window, and cross-platform transfer unproven in both directions.

This cluster is a *research method*, and it is the most transferable content in the

*STATED, PERFORMANCE, DEMONSTRATED-as-estimate.*

> **Two calibration warnings.** (a) The tool is **the speaker's own product** and its
> outputs are estimates, not account data; he opens by saying he built it because
> rival tools are "wildly inaccurate." Self-interested and unverifiable. The revenue
> figure stays **CLAIMED**, not demonstrated. (b) He narrates "48% of their traffic

> **I could not verify either way, and am not asserting a discrepancy.**

**6.2 The direct-traffic inference — flagged as methodologically weak.** He reads a
high *direct* traffic share as evidence of ad clicks ("someone clicked on the link
from their ad"), then reasons: heavy traffic → running ads → making money.
*STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, ASSERTED.*

> **PHC synthesis (INFERENCE): treat this inference as unreliable.** Paid clicks
> normally carry a referrer or UTM and are classified as paid or social, not direct;
> "direct" is the bucket for missing-referrer traffic. And "traffic implies ads
> implies profit" skips CAC entirely — a store can buy heavy traffic and lose money

> profitability signal. If any of this reaches Augur, it should be filed with the

## 7. ETHICS FILTER — REJECTED TACTICS

**This section is unusually long, and that is the headline finding.** The store the
speaker holds up as "the golden formula for high-converting women's clothing brand
websites" is load-bearing on tactics PHC has banned. Every item below was screened
against PHC standing rules (no fake urgency or scarcity, no fabricated testimonials,
no manufactured social proof, honest reason-why for any deadline or discount).

**7.1 Fabricated reviews carrying "Verified" badges. REJECTED — most serious item.**
[22:19–22:59] Frame-verified at 22:55 on the Almond Muse page: named reviewers
(Zia Q., Jasmine M., Sofie A.), five-star ratings, customer photos in the garment,
each marked **"✓ Verified"**. The speaker states plainly: "none of these customers
bought this dress from this product. None of them wrote these reviews. These are all
completely fake." *Two* violations — fabricated testimonials, plus an affirmative
false claim of verification. Not adoptable in any form.

**7.2 False brand history. REJECTED.** A "20th Anniversary Sale" run by a store whose
own footer reads "2026 Barber Denver Boutique." The speaker notices the impossibility
and dismisses it: "I don't know about 20th anniversary sale. Anyways, it doesn't
matter. That's just part of e-commerce." It is a fabricated reason-why, which is
exactly what PHC's honest-reason-why rule prohibits.

**7.3 Fake stock scarcity. REJECTED.** "Only a few items left," "only four dresses left
in stock," "almost sold out" — on **dropshipped goods the seller holds no inventory
of**. The scarcity is not merely unverified, it is structurally false.

**7.4 Fabricated demand pressure. REJECTED.** "Due to overwhelming support during our
20th anniversary sale, only a few units remain at the current price." Manufactured
social proof plus manufactured scarcity in one sentence.

**7.5 Rolling deadline on an evergreen discount. REJECTED.** "For the next 24 hours
only… this sale ends today," on a permanent promotion, which the speaker approves of
as "hammering it into the customer's head." A deadline that does not exist.

**7.6 Checkout countdown timer. REJECTED.** Frame-verified at 11:40: "**09:49 Until
your order expires**" above a $229.79 order summary. No order is reserved and nothing
expires. Fake urgency at the highest-intent moment on the site.

**7.7 Misrepresentative "as seen on" imagery. REJECTED.** [18:30–20:22] The UGC block
shows women wearing visually similar dresses that are, per the speaker's own
side-by-side of bodice construction, **not the garment being sold** — "if I had to
guess, none of these dresses are the actual product that this brand is selling." He
spends ninety seconds proving the mismatch and treats it as normal. Misrepresentation
of the product.

**7.8 AI-generated imagery passed off as product photography. REJECTED.** [06:55–07:47]
Frame-verified artifacts: floors morphing into walls, a railing passing through
concrete, "a stairway to nothing," inconsistent colourways. Defended on detectability
— "normal people don't know what AI generated images look like." Detectability is not
the test; whether the image represents the product is. **Note the salvageable part:**
the *artifact catalogue* (geometry continuity failures, background morphing, variant
inconsistency) is a genuinely useful QA checklist for auditing AI-generated assets —
keep the tells, reject the practice.

**7.9 Predetermined "scratch to win" popup. REJECTED.** [23:06–23:14] A game-of-chance
frame on a discount every visitor receives, gating it behind an email capture. Fake
randomness.

**7.10 Unsubstantiated price anchoring. REJECTED.** "$99, on sale from $200"; "up to
80% off" — with no evidence any unit ever sold at the anchor. PHC bans fake price
anchoring by name.

**7.11 Unsubstantiated competitor disparagement. REJECTED (copy only).** The us-vs-them
block asserts rival dresses "fit terrible" and are "scratchy." The *structure* is
keepable (§2.6); these claims are invented.

**7.12 Design copying and image appropriation. REJECTED — legal, not only ethical.**
The model is knowingly selling factory knockoffs of a designer original (trade-dress
and design-right exposure), and the speaker notes in passing that one competitor
"stole these images from a different brand" (copyright). §5.3's TikTok-creative
harvesting has the same defect. Route the exposure to **Praetorian** (unbuilt) as
well as flagging it here.

**7.13 Returns framed as a forgetfulness bet. REJECTED (intent only).** [08:45–09:07]
"Literally 90% of the time they just forget to return it and then the 30-day window
passes and they have to keep both. So it's basically the perfect scenario." Revenue
predicated on the customer failing to act is incompatible with "feel like family, not
a transaction." Keep the mechanic from §3.3; reject this as its rationale.

**7.14 Engineered shipping-time ambiguity. REJECTED — and it is worse than stated.**
[20:23–21:12] The speaker praises stating "USA 5 to 8 business days" plus "24-hour
dispatch" because business days convert to 14 calendar days while *reading* shorter —
"one way to make the shipping time seem lower but actually be a little bit longer."
That is deliberate obfuscation of a material term.

> **And it does not reconcile.** The AutoDS supplier cards frame-verified at 13:28
> show **11–14 business days** shipping for this garment. A storefront promising
> **5–8 business days** on a product whose only shown supplier takes 11–14 business
> days is not merely vague — the promise appears **undeliverable on its face**. Both
> numbers appear on screen in the same video. This is the single most concrete
> verifiable defect in the teardown and the speaker does not notice it.
