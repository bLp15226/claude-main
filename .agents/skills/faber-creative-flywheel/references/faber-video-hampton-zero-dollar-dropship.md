## 1. Section-structure transfer — the closest legitimate case to Faber's line

**1.1 What the source does.** [11:36–12:23] His argument: the winning competitors *"already
did our homework… they've already spent months figuring out exactly which page sections get
people to buy."* So he screenshots the best competitor's page sections, drops the screenshots
into an LLM, and instructs it to **"build me these same sections for my brand and my
messaging."** The stated result: *"the same exact selling structure based off of something
that's already working in the market, but for our brand and in our words."* His summary:
*"don't reinvent the wheel… I just borrowed the strategy."*
*STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, DEMONSTRATED.*

**1.2 Why this earns a place in Faber rather than a rejection.** Faber's rule, promoted to
`SKILL.md` at v1.2.0 (2026-07-28): *"Analyse structure. Never replicate a specific creator's
execution beat-for-beat."*

This case sits on the **permitted** side, and it is the closest thing to the line that has
come through intake. What travels is *which sections a page uses and in what order* — a fact
about how the market sells the category. What does not travel is any of their material: the
copy is regenerated from PHC's own messaging, the brand is PHC's, the words are PHC's.

**1.3 The calibration pair — this is the actual contribution.** Placed against the previous
intake, the two cases bracket the rule from both sides:

| Case | What was taken | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **This source** (§1.1) | Which sections a page uses, in what order — rebuilt with own brand, own messaging, own words | **Permitted.** Structure abstracted; nothing of theirs ships. |
| **"One Person Business"** thumbnails (intake filed 2026-07-29, `faber-video-oneperson-faceless-youtube.md` §3.2) | The competitor's **actual thumbnail file**, downloaded via a grabber and edited | **Over the line.** Their artefact ships, modified. |

> **THE OPERATIVE LINE (PHC synthesis, INFERENCE).**
>
> **Screenshots may inform what you build. They may not become what you ship.**
>
> The test is not *how much* was borrowed — it is **whether any of their artefact survives
> into your output.** Section order is a fact about the market and cannot be owned. A JPEG
> is their property. Both cases involve looking hard at a competitor's work; only one
> involves shipping it.
>
> This resolves the ambiguity the existing rule leaves. "Never replicate beat-for-beat"
> tells an operator what not to do without telling them where analysis ends — and §1.1 is
> exactly the case a cautious reading would wrongly refuse. The pair supplies the boundary.

**1.4 One caution on the prompt as phrased.** *"Build me these same sections"* is an
instruction that invites replication. What keeps this source's output on the right side is
the surrounding practice — own brand, own messaging, and the approve-every-line discipline
recorded at `../../scriptor/references/scriptor-video-hampton-zero-dollar-dropship.md` §2.3.
Handed to someone without that discipline, the same prompt produces a replica. **The prompt
is not the safeguard; the practice around it is.** Any Faber brief that borrows structure
states explicitly that copy is regenerated, not adapted.

## 2. Decomposing a winning short-form ad

**2.1 The three dimensions.** [14:14–14:41] He pastes a winning video's URL into a tool that
*"rips the video apart and tells me exactly why it's winning"* — and names the axes it reports:
**hook, visual proof, pacing**. The tool then returns the formula.
*STATED, PLATFORM MECHANICS, DEMONSTRATED.*

**2.2 What survives without the tool, which is the useful part.** The three axes are
tool-independent and directly usable in a brief. A short-form ad that works can be read as:

- **Hook** — what happens in the first beat that stops the scroll.
- **Visual proof** — what the viewer *sees* that makes the claim credible without narration.
  This is the axis most often missing from a brief, and the one an AI-generated asset is
  least likely to supply honestly.
- **Pacing** — the cut rhythm, and how quickly the piece reaches the thing it is about.

> **PHC synthesis (INFERENCE) — why the decomposition matters more than the diagnosis.**
> Faber already varies hooks narrowly on winners and widely on potentials. Naming *three*
> axes rather than one gives the flywheel more than one dimension to vary along — and,
> more usefully, it makes the **confound explicit**: if a variation changes hook *and*
> pacing at once, the result is unreadable regardless of how it performs. That is the same
> logic as the existing bloat guard, one level down: variation that cannot produce a
> readable answer is waste, not testing.
>
> **Vary one axis at a time, and name which one in the brief.**

**2.3 Where this stops.** The source uses these axes to reverse-engineer a *specific*
competitor's video, then generate against its formula. Per §1.3, reading the axes across
several performers is analysis; regenerating one identified creator's video from its decoded
formula is the replica case. **The axes are adopted; single-source formula cloning is not.**

## 3. Ethics filter — asset-side

**3.1 AI-generated "creators" — REJECTED as doctrine, already covered, stated precisely.**
He describes picking *"one of their AI creators"* and says the render *"looks real. It sounds
real."* An AI-generated human presented as a real endorser is a fabricated testimonial.

> **ALREADY COVERED — no new rule.** `faber-creative-flywheel/SKILL.md:242` already lists as
> an anti-pattern *"Any AI-generated testimonial, review, or customer-voice claim presented
> as real experience."* This speaker's **prior video was already rejected on the same
> ground** (§7.1 of the Legatus Hampton file). **Second instance from the same speaker** —
> corroboration for a rule PHC holds, not a gap. Ruled: no new rule needed.
>
> **Precision on the evidence, because overclaiming here would be the same error the rule
> exists to prevent.** The frames sampled at the generation step (15:00) show
> **Mode: "Product Ad"** and a **product-only cutout render**, and the creative actually
> posted (16:50) is a product-only cinematic shot with **no human in it**. So a fake human
> endorsement is **narrated but not frame-confirmed** in this sample. Graded **ASSERTED, not
> DEMONSTRATED.** The standing rejection is unaffected; the instance is not claimed as proven.

**3.2 Cosmetic efficacy claims — risk note, logged not routed.** The product is an
anti-ageing "3D lifting" neck cream, and an on-screen tile claims it *"tightens your face…
making you look younger instantly."* For Faber specifically this is a **creative-brief
constraint**: any asset produced for a product in a regulated claim category inherits that
product's substantiation burden, and a generated asset makes unsubstantiated claims
frictionlessly. Logged as the third entry on the risk pile. **Ruled 2026-07-29: the
Praetorian trigger is NOT met** — the standing rule was same-category recurrence and the
three entries are three distinct harm types.

**3.3 Minor inconsistency, recorded for completeness.** He says the ad generated *"in
seconds"*; the tool's own on-screen text (frame-verified 15:00) reads *"Product ads typically
take 4-10 minutes to render."* *CLAIMED, inconsistent.* Bears only on tool-speed claims.

**3.4 Source-reliability caveat, second instance.** Undisclosed affiliate relationships
throughout, corroborating the caveat already logged against this speaker. Offset by an
unusually honest cost disclosure — he states that a recommended tool costs money, gives the
free manual path in full, and sets expectations against his own interest.
