## 1. AI-generated asset QA — a production-time gate

**Ruled: approved as-is. Clean production-time QA gate.**

This is the inversion of the source's own position. The speaker catalogues the flaws in
a competitor's AI-generated product imagery in detail, then concludes they do not
matter because "normal people don't know what AI generated images look like." PHC
rejects the conclusion (§3.1) and keeps the catalogue: **the tells he enumerates are a
usable checklist for auditing our own generated assets before they ship.**

**1.1 The artifact tells.** [06:55–07:47, 10:17–10:29, 14:55–15:04, 24:15–24:29]
Frame-verified at 07:07 and across the copycat stores. Each item below is something he
identified on screen:

1. **Geometry discontinuity at surface boundaries** — a floor that "just morphs into the
   wall," with no seam or edge where two planes should meet. His most-repeated tell,
   found on multiple images across multiple stores.
2. **Objects passing through solid structure** — a railing that "actually goes through
   the concrete wall."
3. **Background elements that change identity mid-frame** — a railing that becomes a
   bush, and a bush that "gets cut off right here, but just becomes a totally different
   bush."
4. **Architecture leading nowhere** — "a stairway to nothing." Structures that imply
   continuation and do not deliver it.
5. **Environment repetition across a gallery** — successive images set in the same
   implausible non-place, with details inconsistent between them.
6. **Variant colourways that were recoloured, not regenerated** — his read on one image
   was that someone said "hey Gemini, make the color pink," and on another, "did bro
   color this in himself?" Flat, unconvincing colour substitution.
7. **Faces** — on the reference store: "I'm sure you can look at this girl's face and
   immediately tell that it was generated with AI."

**1.2 Where the failures cluster (PHC synthesis, INFERENCE).** Six of the seven tells are
not about the subject at all — they are **background and environment failures**. The
garment renders convincingly; the world around it does not. The practical consequence
for briefing and reviewing generated assets: **audit the background first**, because
that is where the artifacts live, and it is the half a reviewer looking at the product
will skip.

Corollary, also ours: artifacts compound across a *set*. A single image survives
scrutiny that a gallery of eight does not, because inconsistency between images (item 5)
is only visible in aggregate. Review generated assets as a set, never one at a time.

**1.3 The QA gate, stated as a checklist.** Before any generated asset ships:

- [ ] Surface boundaries — do floors, walls and ceilings meet at real edges?
- [ ] Occlusion — does any object pass through solid structure?
- [ ] Object identity — does anything change what it is between one side of the frame and the other?
- [ ] Implied continuation — do stairs, paths, railings and doorways lead somewhere?
- [ ] Set consistency — reviewed as a gallery: is the environment coherent across all images?
- [ ] Variants — were colourways regenerated, or recoloured?
- [ ] Faces and hands, at full resolution.
- [ ] **Representation test (the non-negotiable one):** does this image depict the
      product a customer will actually receive? See §3.1.

**1.4 What this checklist is not.** It is a *quality* gate, not a licence. Passing it
means the asset has no visible artifacts. It says nothing about whether generated
imagery is an honest depiction of the product — that is a separate and stricter test,
and it is the one the source fails (§3.1).

## 2. Cross-note — creative complexity should scale to explanation burden

**Scriptor is primary owner (see `scriptor-video-zuco-ai-dropship-teardown.md` §3).
Recorded here for creative-brief use only.**

For a visually self-evident product, the ad is the product photograph plus price plus
offer — no mechanism, no benefit stack. Frame-verified at 04:18: the live ad running
against a ~$1.2M/month store is a single full-body product photo with a sale headline
and no body copy. The speaker's contrast case is supplements, where the ad must carry
the mechanism and the benefit.

**Brief-side consequence:** the first question on a creative brief is *how much does
this product need explained before someone wants it?* — because the answer sets the
creative's shape. A self-evident product briefed with heavy mechanism copy wastes the
frame; a mechanism-dependent product briefed as "just show it" fails outright, and
that is the more expensive error of the two.

**Authority note:** per Axis 1 the book stack governs the underlying principle
(sophistication stage). This heuristic is a triage shortcut feeding that diagnosis, and
is explicitly subordinate to it. Do not let it override a stage diagnosis.

## 3. ETHICS FILTER — asset-side rejections

All 14 rejections from this source approved 2026-07-29. Copy-side items are recorded in
the Scriptor file §5. The asset-side items:

**3.1 AI imagery passed off as photography of the actual product. REJECTED (practice).**
The speaker's defence is detectability: "normal people don't know what AI generated
images look like… no one even looks at the reviews. Like, people don't care."

> **Detectability is not the test.** Whether the image represents the product the buyer
> will receive is the test. A flawless generated image of a garment nobody will receive
> is *more* deceptive than a flawed one, not less — the artifacts are the only thing
> currently protecting the customer. **The tells in §1 are kept as a QA checklist for
> our own assets. The practice of substituting generated imagery for the real product
> is rejected outright**, and §1.3's final checklist item exists to enforce exactly
> that boundary.

**3.2 Misrepresentative "as seen on" imagery. REJECTED.** The reference store's UGC block
shows women wearing visually similar dresses which are, by the speaker's own
side-by-side of bodice construction, not the garment being sold. He proves the mismatch
and treats it as routine. Faber's constraint is unchanged: **no fabricated proof**, and
a photograph of a different product used as proof of this one is fabricated proof.

**3.3 Third-party TikTok creative harvesting. REJECTED — not staged as doctrine.**
[26:34–26:55] The method: search "<product name> TikTok," find existing organic videos
of the same physical product, use them as raw material for your own creatives.
Frame-verified — a results grid of many videos of the same dress.

> **Ruled rejected (item 10):** the video never addresses rights or permission, and an
> unresolved legal question is not something to bake into Faber. **Logged as an open
> question only** in the intake disposition record. Not adopted, not staged, no
> proposal raised. If it is ever revisited it needs a licensing step attached that
